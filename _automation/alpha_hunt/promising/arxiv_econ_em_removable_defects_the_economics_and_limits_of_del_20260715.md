## Summary
This is a theoretical economics paper about when it pays a specialist to deliberately keep
a "blind spot" instead of eliminating it, and when that blind spot should instead be routed
to a compensating detector. The authors frame this as a market-vs-self-insurance tradeoff
(applied to a competence gap) combined with a costly-state-verification detector, and prove
two main results: a condition under which keeping the deficiency is economically rational,
and a two-sided characterization of when a detector can profitably "remove" it on demand —
including a converse showing that a confounded detector earns zero premium and, under
compounding (multiplicative) dynamics, drives long-run growth negative. They separately
treat "observation defects" (fixable by seeing more) versus "capacity defects" (not fixable
by more data), and connect the framework to Chow's reject option, Kelly criterion growth
under ruin, and selective prediction.
